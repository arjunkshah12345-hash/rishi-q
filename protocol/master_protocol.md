RISHI-Q
A Blinded Computational Test of Quantum-Structural Analogues in Classical Sanskrit Thought
Master Research Protocol v1.0Study type: Computational humanities × history/philosophy of physics × NLP × statistical inferenceCompute: MacBook Air + Kaggle Cloud onlyIntended outputs: Open-source research repository, preregistered experiment, reproducible dataset, arXiv preprint, interactive research explorerStatus: Pre-experimental design

0. Executive Summary
RISHI-Q asks a simple but controversial question:
Do classical Sanskrit philosophical texts contain structural descriptions of reality that resemble modern quantum mechanics or quantum field theory to a statistically unusual degree?
Claims connecting concepts such as prāṇa, ākāśa, śakti, Brahman, spanda, aṇu/paramāṇu, observation, manifestation, unity, vibration, and causation with quantum physics are widespread.
Most such comparisons suffer from a fundamental methodological problem:
We already know quantum mechanics and can retrospectively reinterpret ambiguous ancient language until it sounds quantum-like.
RISHI-Q is designed specifically to test whether the alleged resemblance survives when retrospective interpretation is made difficult.
The study will:
construct a historically documented corpus of Sanskrit philosophical passages;
construct matched control corpora from other philosophical traditions;
create a physics-derived structural ontology before examining the confirmatory Sanskrit corpus;
anonymously annotate passages for explicit structural propositions;
distinguish generic metaphysical ideas from classical field-like concepts and genuinely quantum-specific concepts;
compare every corpus against fingerprints of multiple physical theories;
test whether Sanskrit traditions show greater quantum-specific correspondence than controls;
repeat the experiment across translations, models and analysis methods;
remove potentially contaminating words such as energy, field, vibration and particle;
use human validation to audit model-generated annotations;
preregister the confirmatory experiment;
publish positive, negative or mixed findings without changing the underlying hypothesis.
The main scientific contribution is therefore not a claim that ancient texts discovered quantum mechanics.
It is a method for answering a more rigorous question:
Can alleged ancient-modern scientific correspondences survive blinded, controlled and falsifiable computational testing?

1. Layman's Version
What are we doing?
People say things like:
“Prāṇa was ancient India's word for energy.”
“Ākāśa was basically the quantum field.”
“Hindu philosophy already knew everything was interconnected.”
“Ancient texts understood the observer effect.”
“Śakti and spanda describe fundamental vibrations.”
Some of those comparisons may contain something interesting.
Some may simply be modern interpretations.
We want to find out which.
Instead of searching scriptures for sentences that sound like quantum physics, we turn passages into structured claims.
For example, imagine an ancient passage says:
All visible forms arise from an underlying reality that persists while the forms themselves change.
Our program does not say:
“Wow! Quantum field theory!”
It records only the structure:
underlying substrate                 YES
observable objects emerge from it    YES
substrate persists through change    YES

discrete allowed states              UNKNOWN
probabilistic outcomes               UNKNOWN
nonseparability                      UNKNOWN
quantization                         UNKNOWN
measurement changes state            UNKNOWN

Now imagine another passage says:
The whole possesses properties that cannot be assigned independently to its components.
That receives:
nonseparability                      POSSIBLY YES

But:
“All things are one”
does not automatically count as quantum entanglement.
Then we do the exact same analysis to:
Indian philosophical texts;
Buddhist texts;
Jain texts;
Greek philosophical texts;
Chinese philosophical texts;
other metaphysical texts;
ordinary Sanskrit controls.
Finally we ask:
Are the Sanskrit target texts actually more quantum-like than these controls?
If not, the exciting hypothesis fails.
If yes, we attempt to destroy the effect with increasingly difficult tests.

2. The Most Important Principle
RISHI-Q must be capable of proving our exciting idea wrong.
A scientific study cannot begin with:
Ancient Hindu scriptures contained quantum physics.
It should begin with:
Some structural similarities have been alleged. Are they statistically unusual under controlled analysis?
We must design an experiment where all of these are legitimate outcomes:
Outcome A
No unusual correspondence exists.
Outcome B
Correspondence exists but is explained by vague metaphysical language.
Outcome C
Correspondence is largely introduced by modern translation.
Outcome D
Some specific philosophical school contains interesting structural analogues.
Outcome E
Several precise quantum-specific structures occur unusually frequently and survive every control.
Every one of these would be a result.

4. Primary Research Question
Do selected classical Sanskrit philosophical corpora exhibit statistically greater structural correspondence with modern quantum mechanics and quantum field theory than appropriately matched historical philosophical control corpora?

5. Primary Thesis
Our neutral research thesis is:
If classical Sanskrit philosophical systems contain unusually strong conceptual structures analogous to modern quantum physics, then a preregistered structural ontology should detect a greater concentration of quantum-specific features in those texts than in matched historical controls, even after controlling for translation modernization, generic metaphysical language, textual clustering, model choice and researcher interpretation.

6. Null and Alternative Hypotheses
H0 — Null Hypothesis
There is no meaningful difference between the quantum-specific structural correspondence of the Sanskrit target corpus and matched historical philosophical controls after prespecified corrections and controls.
Formally:
[H_0:\Delta_Q \leq 0]
where:
[
\Delta_Q =
E(QS_{\text{target}})
E(QS_{\text{control}})]

H1 — Primary Alternative Hypothesis
The Sanskrit target corpus exhibits significantly greater quantum-specific structural correspondence than matched historical controls:
[H_1:\Delta_Q > 0]
The important word is:
quantum-specific.
Generic statements about:
unity;
change;
hidden reality;
interconnectedness;
consciousness;
vibration;
cannot by themselves establish H1.

7. Secondary Hypotheses
H2 — Quantum Specificity
Target texts should match QM/QFT more strongly than classical physical theories.

H3 — Translation Robustness
Any observed effect should survive older and more literal translations.

H4 — Vocabulary Robustness
The effect should survive removal or neutralization of modern physics vocabulary.

H5 — Cross-Model Robustness
The result should not depend on one embedding or language model.

H6 — Human Reproducibility
Independent human annotators should reproduce the main ontology assignments with acceptable reliability.

H7 — School Specificity
Different Sanskrit philosophical traditions may have significantly different physical-ontology fingerprints.

H8 — Feature Specificity
Any positive result should be explainable by identifiable structural features rather than one generic “similarity score.”

8. The Three Levels of Similarity

LEVEL I — Generic Metaphysical Similarity
Examples:

More specific concepts include:

local states of a distributed entity;
propagation;
oscillation;
transformation laws;
interactions transmitted through a medium/substrate;

These may resemble ideas from:

wave mechanics;
continuum mechanics;
without being quantum.
This distinction is critical for concepts such as ākāśa, prāṇa, śakti and spanda.

Much stronger features include:

contextual dependence of observations;
incompatible observables;
state reduction/measurement interaction;
superposition-like state structure;
quantized excitations;
field excitations corresponding to particle-like objects.
These features receive the greatest evidentiary importance.

14. Physics Ontology
Create approximately 35–50 final features.
The exact ontology will be developed using the development corpus and then frozen before confirmatory testing.
Initial feature groups:

A. Fundamental Structure
O01 — Fundamental substrate
An underlying entity or level is presented as ontologically more fundamental than observable objects.
O02 — Constituents
Macroscopic objects are composed from more elementary entities.
O03 — Fundamental discreteness
Fundamental constituents/states are discrete.
O04 — Continuous substrate
A distributed continuous entity is described.
O05 — Local manifestation
A broader substrate has localized manifestations.
O06 — Emergence
Higher-level properties arise from lower-level organization.

15. Dynamics
D01 — State
An entity can occupy distinguishable configurations.
D02 — Transformation
States change according to identifiable relationships.
D03 — Periodicity
Dynamics are explicitly cyclic/oscillatory.
D04 — Propagation
A disturbance or influence propagates.
D05 — Conservation
Some quantity/property remains invariant through change.
D06 — Symmetry
The structure is invariant under specified transformations.
D07 — Reversibility
A transformation can formally reverse.

16. Quantum-Specific Features
Q01 — Discrete allowed states
Only certain physical states/values are permitted.
Q02 — Superposed possibilities
Multiple alternative states are simultaneously represented prior to outcome selection.
Q03 — Fundamental probability
Probability is inherent in physical prediction rather than merely reflecting ignorance.
Q04 — Measurement-conditioned outcome
The outcome is associated with a measurement interaction.
Q05 — Incompatible observables
Two properties cannot simultaneously possess arbitrary definite values.
Q06 — Nonseparability
A composite state cannot be represented as independent component states.
Q07 — Context dependence
Possible outcomes depend structurally upon measurement context.
Q08 — Quantized excitation
Localized entities correspond to discrete excitations of an underlying distributed system.

17. Relational Features
R01 — Local causation
Effects proceed through local interactions.
R02 — Long-range relation
Separated entities remain explicitly correlated.
R03 — Holistic state
The state of a whole contains information not reducible to independent component descriptions.
R04 — Relational properties
Properties are defined relative to other entities rather than absolutely.

18. Observer / Epistemic Features
M01 — Observer/system distinction
An observer and observed system are explicitly distinguished.
M02 — Observation modifies system
The act of measurement/observation physically changes the system.
M03 — Epistemic limitation
The observer cannot know some property.
This must remain distinct from physical indeterminacy.
M04 — Ontic indeterminacy
The property itself is not specified as having one predetermined value prior to the relevant interaction.

19. Field-Like Features
This section directly addresses your original interest in “energy fields.”
F01 — Spatial distribution
An entity exists across an extended spatial region.
F02 — Local state of distributed entity
Different locations may possess different states/values.
F03 — Disturbance propagation
Changes propagate through the distributed entity.
F04 — Localized manifestation
Localized observable phenomena arise from that distributed entity.
F05 — Interaction mediation
The distributed entity mediates interactions.
F06 — Excitation-like structure
Localized entities correspond to dynamically changed states of the underlying structure.
F07 — Quantization of distributed structure
The distributed entity permits explicitly discrete excitations/states.
Notice the hierarchy.
A text satisfying F01–F05 may possess something field-like.
Only when something like F07 appears does the analogy become specifically quantum-field-like.

23. Translation Is an Experimental Variable
Do not treat translation as invisible preprocessing.
Where feasible, collect multiple versions:
T1
Original Sanskrit.
T2
Conservative/literal scholarly translation.
T3
Historically older English translation.
T4
Recent scholarly translation.
T5
Machine-generated translation, secondary analysis only.
Recent Sanskrit NLP resources are increasingly capable, but even modern large-scale datasets explicitly note difficulties with philosophical concepts, compounds and multilayered metaphor. The 2026 Mitrasamgraha dataset contains 391,548 Sanskrit–English pairs and specifically highlights these translation challenges.
Therefore machine translation should never silently become the textual authority.

26. Why We Do Not Use Embeddings as the Primary Test
A naive study might do:
cosine_similarity(upanishad, quantum_physics)

That is not enough.
Embedding models contain learned associations and may themselves have seen writing connecting Indian philosophy with quantum mechanics.
Recent work on LLM-assisted annotation also demonstrates that even human-approved model-generated labels can alter label distributions and downstream conclusions.
Therefore:
The primary result must come from explicit, auditable ontology labels.
Embeddings become a robustness experiment.

38. Structural Similarity
For ancient passage (i):
[X_i =(x_1,x_2,...,x_K)]
Compute similarity to each theory.
Primary metric candidate:
Weighted Jaccard similarity over explicit features
[
S(X,T)
\frac{\sum_k w_k\min(X_k,T_k)}{\sum_k w_k\max(X_k,T_k)}]
with missing values handled according to a preregistered rule.
Weights must be fixed independently from the target data.

39. Quantum Specificity Score
A passage that resembles all theories is not specifically quantum.
Define:
[
QS_i =
S(X_i,Q)
\max_{C\in Classical}S(X_i,C)]
where (Q) may be a preregistered QM/QFT composite.
Interpretation:
QS > 0
The structure resembles quantum theories more than its strongest classical alternative.
QS ≈ 0
No meaningful specificity.
QS < 0
It resembles a classical theory more strongly.
This is far more informative than:
“sounds like quantum physics.”

[\Delta_{observed}]

82. Falsification Conditions
We should consider the exciting hypothesis unsupported if:
target/control difference is negligible;
confidence intervals include only scientifically trivial effects;
translation masking removes the effect;
generic metaphysical controls perform equally well;
classical field theory explains the resemblance as well as QM/QFT;
human annotation does not reproduce labels;
one model family causes most of the effect;
one source text creates the entire effect;
results fail cluster-aware inference;
quantum-specific features remain rare.

83. What Counts as an Interesting Positive Result?
A strong positive case would look something like:
target-control difference substantial;
confidence interval excludes trivial effects;
survives cluster-aware testing;
survives old/literal translations;
survives terminology masking;
survives removing generic “oneness” language;
survives model changes;
human annotation agrees;
specifically favors QM/QFT over classical field theory;
arises across multiple independent Sanskrit texts;
is driven by several precise features.
Only then should the discussion become ambitious.

84. Interpretation Ladder
Use increasingly strong conclusions only when evidence permits them.
Level 1
A textual similarity exists.
Level 2
A structural analogy exists.
Level 3
The structural analogy is statistically enriched relative to historical controls.

The enrichment is specifically closer to quantum than classical physical theories.

Multiple independent quantum-specific structures are present.
Even Level 5 does not establish:

100. Success Criteria for the Project

101. The Most Exciting Possible Result
The most interesting credible outcome is not:
“Everything in quantum physics was in the Vedas.”
That would immediately look unserious.
A far more powerful outcome would be something like:
“Across a preregistered corpus and matched historical controls, selected Sanskrit philosophical traditions showed no unusual correspondence on generic quantum metaphors, but exhibited a reproducible enrichment of three specific structural motifs—X, Y and Z. The effect survived literal translation, vocabulary masking, human annotation and cross-model replication and was significantly stronger for QM/QFT fingerprints than for classical field theory.”
Now there is something genuinely unusual to explain.

102. The Most Interesting Negative Result
Imagine instead:
Modern translations scored dramatically higher than literal/older translations, while the difference between Sanskrit and matched mystical controls disappeared after masking the words energy, vibration and field.
That would quantitatively demonstrate how modern “quantum mysticism” may be created.
That is also a compelling scientific paper.

103. The Research Standard
Before every methodological choice ask:
Would I make this same decision if the result made the Sanskrit corpus look less quantum-like?
If no:
do not make the decision.
Before every conclusion ask:
Could this result also arise from generic metaphor, translation bias, annotation bias or classical physics?
If yes:
test that alternative.
Before publication ask:
Could a skeptical physicist reproduce this conclusion from our data without accepting any spiritual premise?
If no:
the study is not ready.

104. Final Research Statement
RISHI-Q will provide a controlled computational framework for evaluating alleged parallels between classical Sanskrit philosophy and modern quantum physics.
Rather than asking whether ancient passages can be interpreted using modern scientific language, the study asks whether their explicit structural propositions exhibit unusual correspondence with quantum-mechanical and quantum-field-theoretic ontologies relative to matched ancient philosophical controls.
The study will separate:
metaphor
from:
field-like structure
from:
quantum-specific structure.
It will measure translation effects, use source-blinded annotation, validate AI labels with humans, control for clustered texts, compare against multiple physical theories, preregister its main hypothesis and subject any positive result to aggressive robustness testing.
The computational architecture requires only:
MacBook Air + Kaggle Cloud.
The MacBook serves as the research, statistics and publication environment.
Kaggle supplies GPU-backed batch inference and simulation when required.
No Raspberry Pi, physical sensors or specialized hardware are necessary.
The goal is not to prove a belief.
The goal is to construct an experiment strong enough that whatever survives it is worth taking seriously.
